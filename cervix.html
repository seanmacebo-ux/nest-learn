Cervix & preterm risk

Doctors may say the cervix (the neck of the womb) opened too early. That can be true, but it's often a diagnosis made by ruling other causes out, so the hospital records matter. There's a clear, evidence-based plan for next time, and most women in this position go on to have a baby.

🧭 The system

The same four questions for every track. Follow the money, then decide.

💸Who pays
  • Public maternity care is funded mainly through taxes and provincial health budgets; the National Health Act provides free public health services for pregnant women who are not medical scheme beneficiaries, subject to its conditions. National Health Act ↗
  • In private care, medical schemes pool members' monthly contributions, while families pay costs outside their cover or above applicable reimbursement rates. How medical schemes work ↗
  • PMBs [prescribed minimum benefits, the legal minimum cover] protect qualifying diagnosis, treatment and care, but provider rules and lawful waiting periods can affect payment. CMS PMB Q&A ↗ CMS pregnancy waiting-period ruling ↗
  • There is no verified universal cerclage price or blanket progesterone funding promise in these sources, so obtain a written decision for the actual diagnosis, procedure, medicine and plan. CMS conditions ↗
⚖️Who decides
  • The patient gives informed consent after the clinician explains the diagnosis, alternatives, benefits, risks and costs, with her partner involved as she wishes. National Health Act, sections 6 and 7 ↗
  • The obstetrician and MFM specialist [maternal-fetal medicine doctor, a specialist in complicated pregnancies] interpret the previous loss and recommend monitoring or treatment. RCOG cervical stitch ↗
  • Public referral teams and hospital capacity influence where specialist care happens, while scheme authorisation teams decide funding under the scheme rules and PMB law. SA maternity guidelines ↗ CMS PMB Q&A ↗
  • Funding disputes can go through the scheme's complaints process and then the Council for Medical Schemes, which regulates schemes. CMS complaints ↗
🏦Who profits
  • Private hospitals, specialist practices, laboratories and medicine suppliers receive payments for the services or products they provide. Health Market Inquiry ↗
  • Medical schemes themselves are nonprofit funds, while a separate commercial administrator can earn fees for managing them. Discovery's explanation of the distinction ↗
  • The 2019 Health Market Inquiry found that fee-for-service [payment for each service] and weak information about quality encouraged excess use across private healthcare. Inquiry findings ↗
  • That finding supports asking why an intervention is needed, but does not prove that a particular clinician's recommendation is financially motivated. SAMJ explanation of the inquiry ↗
  • SA reporting also identifies litigation fears and scheduling pressures in maternity decisions, so financial incentives are only part of the picture. Bhekisisa maternity investigation ↗
🫶What it means for us
  • Arrange a specialist review of the 25-week loss before another pregnancy if possible, because painless cervical opening, labour, infection and waters breaking first can lead to different prevention plans. Mayo diagnosis and treatment ↗
  • Ask the specialist to explain whether the next pregnancy needs an early planned stitch or cervical scans with a treatment trigger, and have that plan written down. RCOG cervical stitch ↗
  • For public care, ask the local clinic or obstetric team to arrange referral to an appropriate high-risk service; Wits lists poor-obstetric-history and perinatal mental-health clinics. Wits MFM division ↗
  • For private care, request an itemised quotation in Rands and written scheme approval covering the surgeon, anaesthetist, facility, scans, medicine and stitch removal separately. Discovery day-surgery rules ↗ CMS PMB Q&A ↗
  • Make grief support part of the care plan, and confirm the next pregnancy-loss group meeting with The Compassionate Friends in Highlands North on 011 440 6322 or WhatsApp 078 147 2705. Organisation's 2026 listing ↗

🧠 Understand it

The basics, in plain words.

1What cervical insufficiency is5 key points
  • The cervix is the neck of the womb. In a normal pregnancy it stays closed and firm until near the end.
  • Cervical insufficiency means the cervix shortens and opens too early. This often happens with no pain at all.
  • You'll hear other names for it: 'incompetent cervix', 'weak cervix', or 'preterm cervical shortening'. They're just labels. None of them means anyone failed.
  • Known causes include being born with it (a differently shaped womb, or a collagen condition such as Ehlers-Danlos), past cervix surgery (LLETZ or cone biopsy after an abnormal smear), or injury in a previous labour. Many women have no known risk factor.
  • No test before pregnancy can reliably predict it. A scan or MRI before pregnancy can find a differently shaped womb, though, and that can be a cause.
2How it's diagnosed (and why to be a bit skeptical)5 key points
  • There's no single test for it. It's usually diagnosed after a late miscarriage or premature birth, once other causes have been ruled out.
  • Mayo Clinic describes the classic picture as the cervix widening without pain (dilation) and the baby being born in the second trimester.
  • Mayo Clinic calls it 'a hard problem to diagnose', especially in a first pregnancy.
  • What happened first matters a lot. If contractions, waters breaking, bleeding or fever came before the cervix opened, infection or the placenta may be the real story.
  • In a future pregnancy, doctors measure the cervix with an internal (transvaginal) scan. A cervix shorter than 25 mm is linked to a higher chance of early birth.
3Other causes of a mid-pregnancy loss6 key points
  • Tommy's says many second-trimester losses are caused by infection. It can reach the baby or the fluid around the baby. When the membranes and fluid are infected, doctors call it chorioamnionitis.
  • Bacterial vaginosis (BV, an imbalance of vaginal bacteria that often causes no symptoms) raises the risk of a late miscarriage.
  • Other possible causes are placenta problems, clotting conditions (antiphospholipid syndrome or thrombophilia), and chromosome problems in the baby.
  • Hospitals can check the placenta and cord under a microscope (histology). They can also run infection tests, blood tests for clotting, thyroid and diabetes, and genetic tests.
  • In a UK study of over 1,000 post-mortems, the cause was found by looking at the placenta, the clinical notes and the baby's examination. Even so, a clear cause was found for only about 4 in 10 babies.
  • Results usually take 6 to 12 weeks, sometimes longer. If no full post-mortem was done, you can still ask for the placenta to be examined.
4What 'higher risk' really means5 key points
  • A big Scottish study followed 65,592 women. Those who lost a pregnancy in the second trimester had a 4.3% chance of a spontaneous premature birth next time, compared with 1.5% for other women.
  • Put the other way round: about 96 in 100 of those women did not have a spontaneous premature birth in their next pregnancy.
  • In the same study, the chance of another second-trimester loss was 3.7% (against 1.1%). Pre-eclampsia (high blood pressure in pregnancy) was about twice as likely (23.4% against 11.7%).
  • These are averages for losses up to 24 weeks. The researchers say the evidence is limited. Your own risk depends on the cause, which is another reason to chase the test results.
  • Treatment helps. A Cochrane review (a gold-standard summary of trials) found that a cervical stitch lowers the chance of early birth in high-risk women. Babies were also less likely to die in their first week.
5What's done next time6 key points
  • If you've had a loss after 16 weeks or a birth before 34 weeks, you should be referred to a specialist early in the next pregnancy.
  • Option 1 is a planned 'history-indicated' stitch (cerclage), put in around 11 to 14 weeks. UK guidance usually saves this for women with 3 or more losses or early births. Mayo Clinic says it can be used after past cervical insufficiency, so ask which applies to you.
  • Option 2 is regular internal scans of cervix length between about 16 and 24 weeks. Mayo Clinic describes these as every two weeks.
  • If the cervix measures 25 mm or less, NICE (the UK guideline body) says to offer a choice: a stitch, or vaginal progesterone (a daily hormone pessary from 16 to 24 weeks until at least 34 weeks). Neither has been shown to beat the other.
  • If a vaginal stitch has failed before, an abdominal stitch (transabdominal cerclage) is placed higher up, ideally before pregnancy. In a UK trial, 8% of women with one gave birth before 32 weeks, against 33% with a standard vaginal stitch. It stays in, so the baby is born by C-section.
  • A vaginal stitch usually comes out at 36 to 37 weeks. An emergency stitch, done after the cervix has already opened, is riskier and less likely to work. That's why a plan in early pregnancy matters.
6Before trying again5 key points
  • There's no fixed waiting time. Tommy's suggests giving your body time to recover and waiting for test results so you go in with as much information as possible.
  • You can get pregnant as early as 3 weeks after giving birth, even before your periods come back. Plan contraception until you're both ready.
  • Start folic acid before you get pregnant, ideally 3 months before. Vitamin D and not smoking help too.
  • See a specialist before the next pregnancy. That could be a maternal-fetal medicine (MFM) subspecialist, an obstetrician trained in high-risk pregnancy. An abdominal stitch, if it's ever needed, is best done before pregnancy.
  • It's common for one partner to feel ready before the other. Talk it through honestly, and get counselling if you need it.

🔬 Deep dive

Past the basics: the evidence, the economics, the misconceptions.

1Reconstruct the loss before naming the cause8 key points
  • A single mid-pregnancy loss does not by itself establish cervical insufficiency [the cervix opening too early without the usual labour process]. Mayo diagnosis ↗
  • Build a timeline from the records showing when pressure, bleeding, contractions, cervical opening and leaking fluid first appeared, because their order helps the specialist assess the mechanism. SASOG-hosted assessment guidance ↗
  • PPROM [waters breaking before labour and before 37 weeks] is a different starting event from painless opening with intact membranes, although these processes can overlap. NICE NG25 ↗
  • Ask for the cervical examination notes, ultrasound reports, infection results, delivery summary and any placental histology [microscope examination of the placenta] to be reviewed together, rather than relying on a discharge label. Loss investigations ↗
  • Cervical tissue changes involve collagen [supporting protein], water content and inflammatory signals, so the biology is more complex than a cervix that simply cannot carry weight. Cervical remodelling review ↗
  • A cerclage [a stitch supporting the cervix] does not treat every pathway to preterm birth, so infection, bleeding and contractions change the decision. UCLH patient guidance ↗
  • There is no reliable test outside pregnancy that proves how the cervix will behave in a future pregnancy. Mayo diagnosis ↗
  • The Aberdeen study defines the previous second-trimester loss as 13 weeks to 23 weeks and 6 days, so its recurrence percentages do not directly describe a previous birth or loss after 24 weeks. PASTeL-2 paper ↗
2Why guidelines can disagree8 key points
  • History-indicated, ultrasound-indicated and examination-indicated stitches are three different decisions based on past events, scan findings or an already opening cervix. FIGO recommendations ↗
  • FIGO recommends a history-based stitch after three or more preterm births or mid-trimester losses, but also recommends a scan-based stitch after one such event when cervical length is under 25 mm. FIGO recommendations ↗
  • Those thresholds do not mean a couple must lose three babies before specialist review or a prevention plan. RCOG early-referral advice ↗
  • NICE allows consideration of vaginal progesterone based on prior spontaneous preterm birth or qualifying loss alone, while ACOG advises against offering it for recurrent preterm birth without a shortened cervix. NICE NG25 ↗ ACOG advisory ↗
  • South Africa's 2024 national maternity guideline describes progesterone as an alternative to cerclage when a short cervix is confirmed before 24 weeks. National maternity guideline ↗
  • SMFM Consult 70 concerns people without previous spontaneous preterm birth, so its recommendation against routine stitches for a closed short cervix cannot simply be applied to someone with a previous spontaneous 25-week birth. SMFM Consult 70 ↗
  • Ask which guideline fits the actual history and why the clinician's recommendation follows or differs from it, because a guideline threshold is a decision aid rather than an individual prognosis. ACOG collaborative planning ↗
  • Treatment-guiding cervical measurements should use a standard transvaginal scan [an ultrasound probe in the vagina], rather than treating an abdominal estimate as interchangeable. SMFM Consult 70 ↗
3Read treatment claims in absolute numbers10 key points
  • The 2018 progesterone analysis found birth before 33 weeks in about 14% with treatment versus 22% with placebo among singleton pregnancies with a short cervix. Individual-patient meta-analysis ↗
  • That is about eight fewer early births per 100 treated in the study population, rather than a promise that progesterone prevents every early birth. Individual-patient meta-analysis ↗
  • A confidence interval [the range showing statistical uncertainty] that crosses no difference means a study has not established a clear benefit for that outcome. Cochrane cerclage review ↗
  • MAVRIC tested abdominal stitches after a previous vaginal stitch had failed, so its strong results support a selected rescue strategy for future pregnancies rather than routine abdominal surgery after any loss. Published trial abstract ↗
  • An abdominal stitch brings surgical risks and usually remains in place, with caesarean birth required while it is present. SMFM Consult 65 ↗
  • SuPPoRT found similar outcomes across three treatments within its chosen equivalence margin [the maximum difference the trial accepted as similar], but was too small to settle uncommon newborn outcomes. SuPPoRT ↗
  • TOPS found no benefit and a mortality concern with a cervical pessary [a silicone support device], in a different population with no prior spontaneous preterm birth. TOPS ↗
  • Combining a stitch with progesterone should have an explicit reason, because these sources do not establish that every high-risk patient benefits from both. SMFM shared-decision recommendation ↗
  • Makena was injectable 17-OHPC [a synthetic progestogen], and its US approval was withdrawn in 2023 after benefit was not confirmed; that decision does not invalidate vaginal progesterone for a short cervix. FDA decision ↗ ACOG advisory ↗
  • Routine bed rest has no demonstrated preventive benefit and can harm physical health, income and mental wellbeing. SMFM activity-restriction guidance ↗
4Turn medical-aid language into a funding decision9 key points
  • A PMB diagnosis must meet its legal descriptor, so an ICD-10 code [a diagnosis billing code] alone does not establish entitlement. CMS conditions ↗
  • A DSP [designated service provider] is the scheme's selected provider for qualifying care, and voluntarily using another provider can trigger a permitted co-payment. CMS PMB Q&A ↗
  • Ask the specialist's practice to submit the clinical indication and exact procedure codes, then ask the scheme to specify which benefit pays each item. CMS coding explanation ↗
  • Ask separately whether the prescribed vaginal progesterone formulation, quantity and duration are covered, including any formulary [approved-medicine list] or exception process. CMS medicine rules ↗
  • The inclusion of cervical cerclage on Discovery's day-surgery list shows that facility rules can matter even when the procedure is recognised. Discovery procedure list ↗
  • An advertised maternity allowance for routine scans does not establish how a medically required series of cervical scans will be funded. Discovery maternity cover ↗
  • A hospital package is not necessarily the full bill: one Mediclinic caesarean page lists R34,255 but excludes doctors, radiology, pathology and independent care for the baby, so use it only as an example of exclusions. Hospital package ↗
  • For a denial, request the written rule and clinical reason, keep the specialist's motivation and accounts, and use the scheme's complaints process before CMS. CMS complaint requirements ↗
  • Joining a scheme while pregnant does not guarantee immediate PMB funding, because lawful waiting periods can apply in some membership histories. CMS 2025 ruling ↗
5Plan the route before the next emergency10 key points
  • Book specialist review early enough to discuss a planned stitch before symptoms or shortening occur. UCLH timing guidance ↗
  • For Edenvale, confirm the public referral destination with the local care team rather than assuming that any academic clinic accepts direct walk-ins. SA referral guidance ↗
  • Wits lists a Poor Obstetrics History clinic and a Perinatal Mental Health clinic, which are useful service descriptions when requesting referral. Wits division ↗
  • In private care, a fetal anatomy scan appointment and an ongoing preterm-birth prevention service may serve different needs, so confirm who will prescribe treatment and act on cervical changes. Park Lane MFM practice services ↗
  • If another pregnancy develops leaking fluid, bleeding, contractions, fever or concerning pelvic pressure, contact maternity triage promptly rather than waiting for the next scheduled scan. RCOG warning signs ↗
  • If very early birth threatens, ask which hospital can provide the required neonatal care and whether transfer before birth is possible. SASOG-hosted preterm-labour protocol ↗
  • At the limits of viability [when survival outside the womb becomes possible], ask the obstetrician and neonatologist for the receiving unit's current policy and outcomes rather than using overseas survival figures as a local promise. SA national guideline ↗
  • NICE permits consideration of emergency cerclage through 27 weeks and 6 days in selected cases with unruptured membranes, so 24 weeks is not a universal cutoff for every type of stitch. NICE emergency-cerclage recommendations ↗
  • Grief and pregnancy planning can move at different speeds for each partner, and counselling can support both without requiring a shared timetable. Tommy's pregnancy-after-loss information ↗
  • The Highlands North organisation has a primary-source pregnancy-loss group listing from August 2026, but its next date still needs confirmation. The Compassionate Friends ↗

✅ What we actually do

Tick them off as you go.

❓ Questions to ask

Screenshot these or read them out.

  1. What exactly makes you think this was the cervix? Did it open without pain, or were there contractions, waters breaking, bleeding or fever first?
  2. Was the placenta sent for histology? Did it show signs of infection (chorioamnionitis) or a placenta problem?
  3. Which infection tests were done, for example swabs for BV? What did they show?
  4. Were tests done for clotting conditions such as antiphospholipid syndrome?
  5. Was a post-mortem or genetic test done or offered? Is it too late to ask for one?
  6. Should I have a scan of my womb's shape before we try again?
  7. Next time, do you recommend a planned stitch at 12 to 14 weeks, or cervix-length scans from 16 weeks? Why that one for me?
  8. Would you add vaginal progesterone? When would I start it, and when would I stop?
  9. If a vaginal stitch doesn't work, would an abdominal stitch be an option? Who does it in Gauteng, and should it be done before pregnancy?
  10. How long do you suggest we wait, and should we see a maternal-fetal medicine specialist first?
  11. Given my history, should my blood pressure be watched more closely next time for pre-eclampsia?

⚠️ Watch out for

Myths, traps and red flags.

  • Bed rest: a Cochrane review found no evidence that it prevents premature birth, and it carries its own harms. The RCOG says bed rest isn't routinely recommended after a stitch.
  • Cervical pessary (a silicone ring placed around the cervix): the evidence is mixed. One early trial looked good, but Mayo Clinic says more research is needed, and it isn't part of the NICE recommendations.
  • A stitch isn't for everyone. It isn't advised with twins, an active infection, bleeding, contractions, or waters that have already broken.
  • Waiting for symptoms is risky. The cervix can open with no pain, and an emergency stitch works less well than a planned one, so get a plan in early pregnancy.
  • Warning signs in a future pregnancy: pelvic pressure, a new backache, mild cramps, a change in discharge (especially smelly or green), bleeding, or fluid leaking. Call the maternity unit, don't wait.
  • Be wary of supplements or 'cervix-strengthening' products sold online. None of the guidelines we checked recommends any.
  • This was not your fault. Tommy's and Miscarriage UK both say a loss like this is very unlikely to be caused by anything you did or didn't do.
