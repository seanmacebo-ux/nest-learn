Five real things in our circle: Mishka's prenatal vitamins, Sean's eczema, his mom's blood pressure, his sister-in-law's acne, and what a blood test actually takes from you. Each one has treatments with solid evidence and a lot of noise around them. The goal is to know enough to ask sharp questions and spot hype.

🧭 The system

The same four questions for every track. Follow the money, then decide.

💸Who pays
  • South African households pay through taxes, medical-scheme contributions and direct payments for consultations, medicines and laboratory tests.
  • Public clinics use state-funded services and medicines bought through public tenders; these prices differ from private pharmacy prices.
  • Hypertension is one of the 26 conditions on the Chronic Disease List [CDL], so qualifying diagnosis, treatment and care fall within prescribed minimum benefits [PMBs] on every medical-scheme option.
  • Schemes can require registration, a formulary [covered medicine list] and designated service providers [DSPs]; choosing another brand or provider can create a co-payment.
  • Eczema and acne are not themselves CDL conditions, so routine cover depends on the option and circumstances rather than an automatic chronic entitlement.
⚖️Who decides
  • SAHPRA assesses medicine safety, quality and effectiveness; registration does not itself decide the medicine's price or whether a scheme pays.
  • The Health Minister and Pricing Committee regulate private-sector medicine pricing and permitted price adjustments, while public procurement uses tenders.
  • Clinicians and patients choose treatment using the diagnosis, severity, other conditions, pregnancy plans and the person's priorities.
  • Medical schemes set formularies and provider networks within legal PMB requirements, and the Council for Medical Schemes oversees compliance.
  • The Department of Health also sets sodium limits for specified processed foods, so food manufacturers help determine salt exposure before anyone reaches for a salt shaker.
🏦Who profits
  • Manufacturers earn revenue from branded and generic medicines; patents and the number of competing suppliers can affect their bargaining power.
  • Pharmacies earn dispensing fees, while distributors receive logistics fees within the medicine supply chain.
  • Andy Gray's Spotlight analysis identifies an extra revenue route when a pharmacy chain owns its wholesaler, and describes limits to public visibility of commercial arrangements.
  • Private clinicians, laboratories, device sellers and skincare companies receive payment for their services or products; a commercial interest does not by itself prove a treatment is ineffective.
  • Medical schemes pool members' money, while administrators and managed-care businesses charge for running services; evaluating their incentives requires separating these organisations.
🫶What it means for us
  • Ask the older parent's scheme for written hypertension registration, its covered medicines, approved pharmacy and consultation and testing rules.
  • Request the medicine's active ingredient, strength, pack size, single exit price [SEP] and dispensing fee so a cheaper generic comparison is meaningful.
  • Use a validated upper-arm monitor with the correct cuff and a clinician-agreed recording plan before interpreting isolated readings.
  • For skin care, judge a simple treatment plan over the agreed weeks using symptoms, photographs and side effects rather than buying a succession of advertised products.
  • For each blood test, ask what question it answers, what result would change care and how results will be explained; pregnancy plans should be discussed before acne medicines are chosen.

🧠 Understand it

The basics, in plain words.

1Prenatal vitamins (Mishka): folate, what's in hers, food first6 key points
  • Folate and folic acid are the same vitamin (B9). Folate is the natural form in food. Folic acid is the man-made form in pills and in SA bread flour and maize meal.
  • Your gut absorbs about half the folate in food, and about 85% of folic acid. Folic acid is the form with the proof that it prevents spine and brain defects (neural tube defects). NIH folate fact sheet ↗
  • Timing matters. The neural tube closes in the first 4 weeks of pregnancy, so folic acid's big job is before conception and in the first trimester. Mishka is past that window now, so the folic acid vs methylfolate choice matters much less this time. For any next pregnancy, start folic acid 3 months before trying.
  • Her current prenatal covers folic acid (500 µg), iodine (150 µg) and B12 well. It's light on DHA (23 mg vs about 200 mg needed), choline (10 mg vs about 450 mg), vitamin D (6.5 µg vs 15 µg) and iron (15 mg vs about 27 mg).
  • No prenatal is fully 'natural'. Even 'whole food' brands get most of their vitamins from lab-made forms, and methylfolate (5-MTHF) is lab-made too. It's just the already-active form.
  • Food can close most of the gaps: 2 to 3 eggs a day for choline (about 150 mg each), tinned pilchards or other oily fish 2 to 3 times a week for DHA, lentils and chickpeas for folate, red meat for iron, and dairy for calcium.
2Eczema (Sean): what it is and the daily routine6 key points
  • Eczema (atopic dermatitis) is two problems at once: a leaky skin barrier and an immune system that overreacts.
  • A weak barrier lets water out and irritants in. Many people carry gene changes (like filaggrin, a skin-building protein) that make the barrier thinner.
  • Itch leads to scratching, which damages skin more, which itches more. Breaking that loop is the whole game.
  • Moisturisers (emollients) are the base treatment, not an extra. The NHS says use them at least twice a day, generously, even when skin looks clear.
  • Soak and seal: short lukewarm bath or shower, pat dry, then put moisturiser on within about 3 minutes so it locks the water in.
  • Common triggers: soap and bubble bath, sweat, heat, wool, stress, dust mites, and fragranced products. Swap soap for an emollient wash.
3Eczema (Sean): medicines that work, and diet myths6 key points
  • Steroid creams (topical corticosteroids) calm flares fast. Used in the right strength, for short bursts, they are safe. Fear of them often causes under-treatment.
  • Dose by fingertip unit (a line of cream from fingertip to first crease). One unit covers about two adult palms of skin.
  • Calcineurin inhibitors (tacrolimus, pimecrolimus) are steroid-free creams. They are good for the face, eyelids and folds where steroids thin the skin.
  • For severe eczema, a dermatologist can offer injections (biologics like dupilumab) or tablets (JAK inhibitors). These work well but are costly in SA, so check your medical aid.
  • Diet: the NHS says do not cut out foods unless a doctor tells you to. Food allergy matters more in babies; in adults, elimination diets rarely help and can backfire.
  • Real evidence: a Cochrane review found moisturisers mean fewer flares and less steroid needed. Most 'miracle' creams were no better than a plain one.
4High blood pressure (Sean's mom): the numbers and why it's silent6 key points
  • Blood pressure has two numbers. The top (systolic) is pressure when the heart squeezes. The bottom (diastolic) is pressure when it rests.
  • Healthy is roughly 90/60 to 120/80. High is 140/90 or more at a clinic, or 135/85 or more at home (NHS).
  • It is called silent because most people feel nothing. WHO says about 44% of adults with high blood pressure do not know they have it.
  • Over years, high pressure damages arteries, which leads to stroke, heart attack, kidney failure and dementia. Treatment cuts those risks.
  • South Africans eat about 6 to 11 g of salt a day. WHO's limit is under 5 g, about one teaspoon. Much of it hides in bread, stock cubes, processed meat and takeaways.
  • SA law has capped salt in processed foods since 2016, which helped, but home cooking and stock cubes still add a lot.
5High blood pressure: what actually lowers it6 key points
  • DASH (Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension) is an eating plan with lots of vegetables, fruit, beans, whole grains and low-fat dairy, and less salt and fatty meat. Trials show it lowers pressure.
  • Exercise helps: aim for about 150 minutes a week of brisk walking or similar.
  • Alcohol raises blood pressure. Cutting down helps; heavy drinking is a big driver.
  • Home checks need a validated upper-arm machine (one tested for accuracy; check the STRIDE BP list). Sit 5 minutes, arm at heart level, take 2 readings morning and evening for a week.
  • Pills matter. Most people need them for life. Stopping because you feel fine can make pressure bounce back up, because feeling fine was never the sign.
  • Emergency signs: very high readings (around 180/120) with chest pain, severe headache, confusion, vision loss or weakness. Face drooping or slurred speech means stroke. Call 112 from a mobile or 10177.
6Acne (sister-in-law): causes, what works, myths and scars6 key points
  • Acne starts when oil (sebum) and dead skin clog a pore, bacteria (C. acnes) grow, and the spot gets inflamed. Hormones drive the oil. It is not caused by dirty skin.
  • First line: benzoyl peroxide (kills bacteria, no resistance) and retinoids like adapalene (unclog pores). Start thin, every other night, and give it 8 to 12 weeks. Azelaic acid is gentler and fades dark marks.
  • Antibiotics should never be used alone or long-term. NICE says pair them with benzoyl peroxide or a retinoid and review by about 3 months to limit resistance.
  • For women, the combined pill or spironolactone can help. Severe or scarring acne needs a dermatologist for isotretinoin, which works very well but needs strict pregnancy prevention and monitoring.
  • Diet: modest evidence that high-GI foods (sugary, white-flour foods that spike blood sugar) and milk, especially skim, worsen acne in some people. Chocolate alone has weak evidence. It's a nudge, not a cure.
  • Picking causes scars; early treatment prevents them. Dark marks (post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation) fade over months with sunscreen. Pitted scars need dermatologist procedures.
7Drawing blood: how much, how fast it comes back, how to read it6 key points
  • An adult has about 5 litres of blood (about 8% of body weight). A test tube holds about 2 to 10 ml, so a normal panel of a few tubes is well under 1% of your blood.
  • A full donation is much bigger: SANBS takes 480 ml, about 10%. Even that is safe for healthy adults.
  • Plasma (the watery part of blood) comes back within a day or two if you drink fluids. Red cells take weeks; haemoglobin is usually back to normal in 6 to 12 weeks.
  • Draws matter when blood is already low: anaemia (too few red cells or low haemoglobin), babies, and long hospital stays where daily tests can add up.
  • FBC (full blood count) basics: haemoglobin (oxygen carrier), MCV (red cell size: small often means low iron, big can mean low B12 or folate), white cells (infection and immunity), platelets (clotting).
  • Reference ranges cover about 95% of healthy people, so a slightly flagged result is not automatically a problem. Trends over time and how you feel matter more.

🔬 Deep dive

Past the basics: the evidence, the economics, the misconceptions.

1Can prenatals harm a pregnancy? What the research actually found10 key points
  • The biggest review found the opposite of harm. Cochrane 2019 pooled 18 trials (91,425 women): multivitamins probably led to slightly fewer preterm births (risk ratio 0.95), though the range just crossed 'no effect'. Cochrane 2019 ↗
  • The controversial one: in the Hungarian trial that proved vitamins prevent neural tube defects, the vitamin group had about 16% more miscarriages. Researchers called it 'terathanasia': vitamins may help the body let go of pregnancies that already had defects, rather than causing healthy ones to fail. Closer monitoring of the vitamin group may also have counted more early losses. Hook & Czeizel 1997, Lancet ↗
  • The same trial's own 1993 write-up said vitamins had no effect on fetal death overall, and found more twins in the vitamin group. Twins carry a higher preterm risk, but this only applies to vitamins taken before conception. Czeizel 1993 ↗
  • The clearest real harm signal is high-dose vitamin C (1,000 mg) plus E (400 IU). In one trial, waters breaking early was about twice as common: PROM 10.6% vs 5.5%, and before 37 weeks 4.6% vs 1.7%. These were high-risk women (high blood pressure or past pre-eclampsia). Her prenatal has 70 mg of C. Spinnato 2008 ↗
  • Iron is U-shaped: too little is clearly linked to preterm birth, and very high stores show up in some observational studies too. Inflammation also raises iron markers, so cause and effect is unclear. Let blood tests decide on extra iron.
  • Vitamin A is settled: preformed vitamin A (retinol) above about 3,000 µg a day can cause birth defects. Hers has 780 µg. Avoid liver and pâté, and don't add another vitamin A supplement.
  • Herbal 'womb tonics', dong quai and high-dose herbs in some 'natural' prenatals can stimulate the uterus and have little safety data. That's a more realistic risk than a standard prenatal.
  • Folic acid and cancer: a 2012 BMJ Open meta-analysis found a borderline increase (RR 1.07), while a 2013 Lancet analysis of about 50,000 people found no significant increase over 5 years. Unmetabolised folic acid shows up in blood at high doses, but no harm in humans has been proven.
  • Folic acid and autism: one Johns Hopkins cohort linked very high folate in the mother's blood at birth to higher autism risk, from a single blood draw. Other studies link normal first-trimester folic acid to lower risk. Normal doses still look protective.
  • High-dose folic acid can hide a B12 deficiency: it fixes the anaemia while nerve damage carries on. That's why the 1,000 µg upper limit applies to folic acid only, not food folate.
2Eczema: a leaky barrier and an immune loop14 key points
  • Eczema involves both impaired skin protection and inflammation [an immune response that causes swelling and irritation].
  • Filaggrin [a protein involved in the skin barrier] helps explain why inherited differences can make skin lose water more easily.
  • Water loss, irritant entry and scratching can reinforce each other, so treating itch alone may leave the loop running.
  • Emollients [medical moisturisers] support the barrier, while topical corticosteroids [anti-inflammatory skin medicines] treat inflammation.
  • The 2017 Cochrane moisturiser review supports fewer flares but does not establish one universally best brand.
  • Cochrane's 2022 steroid review found that once-daily potent steroid treatment probably works as well as more frequent application for flares.
  • A clinician may prescribe intermittent treatment on recurring sites after a flare settles; this is called proactive maintenance.
  • Steroid strength, body site and duration matter more than a blanket claim that all steroids are dangerous or harmless.
  • The UK medicines regulator recognises severe withdrawal reactions, thought to be rare, but says their true frequency is unknown.
  • A positive allergy test can show sensitisation [immune recognition] without proving that eating the food causes symptoms.
  • AFSA advises expert assessment before food exclusions, especially because restrictive diets can create nutritional harm.
  • Painful blisters, rapidly worsening eczema, fever or feeling ill need prompt medical assessment for infection, including eczema herpeticum [a herpes infection of eczema skin].
  • Inflammation can look purple, grey or darker brown on darker skin, so redness alone is a poor measure of severity.
  • Persistent disease despite a well-used plan can justify reassessing the diagnosis or discussing specialist treatments; US guideline recommendations do not establish South African availability or funding.
3An older parent's blood pressure: measurement before targets14 key points
  • Systolic pressure [the upper number] reflects pressure when the heart contracts, while diastolic pressure [the lower number] reflects pressure between beats.
  • Age-related artery stiffness can raise the upper number even when the lower number is not high.
  • A stressful clinic visit can raise pressure temporarily; white-coat hypertension means clinic readings are high while out-of-clinic readings are lower.
  • Masked hypertension is the opposite pattern, with apparently normal clinic readings but high readings elsewhere.
  • A validated device, correct cuff and consistent seated technique help prevent measurement error from becoming a diagnosis.
  • US categories in Mayo's chart start stage 1 hypertension at 130/80, while NICE confirmation uses clinic readings of at least 140/90 and a home or daytime average of at least 135/85.
  • A diagnosis threshold and a treatment target answer different questions, so ask which local guideline and measurement setting the clinician is using.
  • The SA Hypertension Society's published 2015 algorithm uses a target below 140/90, or below 150/90 above age 80; its date matters when comparing newer guidance.
  • NICE recommends checking standing pressure in people aged 80 or over and in those with relevant symptoms, because treatment can worsen postural hypotension [a pressure drop on standing].
  • An older person's target should account for frailty, falls, kidney function and treatment tolerance rather than age alone.
  • The SPRINT subgroup aged 75 or over benefited from intensive treatment, but it excluded important groups such as people with diabetes, prior stroke or nursing-home residence.
  • Diuretics [medicines that increase salt and water excretion], calcium-channel blockers and medicines acting on blood-pressure hormones lower pressure through different routes.
  • Kidney function and blood salts may need monitoring when medicines are started or changed; a normal reading on treatment is not a reason to stop it independently.
  • Very high repeated readings need prompt clinical advice; chest pain, new weakness, confusion or severe breathlessness require emergency assessment rather than waiting for a home diary.
4Acne: blocked follicles, hormones and slow evidence15 key points
  • Acne develops in the hair-follicle and oil-gland unit through blockage, oil production, bacteria and inflammation.
  • A comedone [blocked follicle] can form a blackhead or whitehead; a blackhead is not evidence of dirty skin.
  • Scrubbing or repeatedly changing strong products can add irritation without fixing the processes that cause acne.
  • Retinoids [vitamin A-related medicines] help prevent blocked follicles, while benzoyl peroxide reduces acne-associated bacteria without being an antibiotic.
  • Combining treatments can target different processes, but several irritating products started together can make the plan hard to tolerate.
  • NICE uses a 12-week first treatment course, because visible improvement commonly takes several weeks.
  • Antibiotics should have a review and stopping plan; AAD recommends benzoyl peroxide alongside antibiotic treatment to limit resistance [bacteria becoming less responsive].
  • Hormonal treatments can help selected women even when acne is not evidence of a dangerous hormone disorder.
  • Acne with irregular periods or other signs of excess androgen activity [effects of hormones such as testosterone] can justify targeted assessment rather than a routine hormone panel for everyone.
  • In the SAFA trial, spironolactone's advantage was clearer at 24 weeks than 12 weeks, showing why an early comparison can miss later benefit.
  • Spironolactone affects blood pressure and potassium as well as hormone signalling, so suitability and monitoring depend on the person's health and other medicines.
  • Pregnancy plans change acne treatment choices: NICE advises against topical retinoids and oral tetracyclines during pregnancy or while planning it.
  • Oral isotretinoin can cause serious fetal harm and requires a carefully supervised pregnancy-prevention and monitoring plan when relevant.
  • NICE finds insufficient evidence to prescribe a particular acne diet; claims that one food explains every breakout overstate the evidence.
  • Scarring, persistent dark marks and psychological distress are valid reasons to seek treatment before acne becomes severe.
5Blood draws: volume, fainting and what results mean15 key points
  • An ordinary diagnostic blood draw removes a small sample from a vein, while a whole-blood donation removes hundreds of millilitres.
  • Mayo describes an adult blood volume of roughly 5,000 mL; as an illustration, a 20 mL collection would be about 0.4% of that volume.
  • The actual volume depends on the requested tests and tubes, so the person taking the sample can explain the planned amount.
  • Fainting around needles can be vasovagal [an automatic nervous-system response that lowers blood pressure and sometimes heart rate], rather than evidence of major blood loss.
  • Tell the collector about previous fainting and ask to lie down if needed.
  • A bruise usually means a little blood leaked into surrounding tissue; applying pressure after needle removal helps limit it.
  • The kidneys release erythropoietin [a signal for red-cell production], and bone marrow makes replacement red cells using nutrients including iron.
  • NHS Blood and Transplant's weeks-long haemoglobin recovery figures describe donation and should not be applied unchanged to a small routine sample.
  • Repeated ICU sampling can contribute to iatrogenic anaemia [low haemoglobin partly caused by medical care], especially in people already seriously ill.
  • STRATUS tested smaller tubes in intensive care; its primary analysis did not show a statistically clear reduction in transfusion, although a larger secondary analysis did.
  • A full blood count measures blood cells but does not directly measure every nutrient or exclude every cause of tiredness.
  • A reference range [the laboratory's comparison interval] is not a boundary between perfect health and disease.
  • Use the units and reference interval on the actual report, and interpret trends alongside symptoms and medicines.
  • Fasting is test-specific, and medicine changes should follow the requesting clinician's instructions rather than a blanket rule.
  • Learning about blood tests cannot establish why the pregnancy loss happened; a cause would require the relevant records and clinical investigation.
6The price of care and the salt already in food16 key points
  • SEP [single exit price] is South Africa's regulated private medicine supply price, not a promise that every pharmacy bill will be identical.
  • A dispensing fee can be added, and medical-scheme reimbursement can differ from the retail amount.
  • Public-sector tender prices use state purchasing power and must not be confused with private SEPs.
  • A regulated annual increase does not prove the starting price is good value; Andy Gray's analysis identifies the initial launch price as a weakness in the framework.
  • Generic comparisons must match the active ingredient, strength and dosage form; two medicines for the same condition are not automatically interchangeable.
  • SAHPRA's bioequivalence framework [assessment of comparable medicine availability in the body] is part of how generic quality and interchangeability are assessed.
  • A brand name, expensive packaging or a long ingredient list is not evidence of better clinical results.
  • For PMBs, distinguish a preference for another brand from a documented medical reason that the formulary option fails or causes harm.
  • Ask for a written funding decision and keep clinical evidence for a formulary exception or scheme complaint.
  • South Africa's sodium rules were introduced in stages in 2016 and 2019 across 13 processed-food categories, making reformulation a population-level prevention measure.
  • The 2025 rural cohort study found estimated sodium excretion fell by about 0.23 g per day from the first to third survey wave; an observational study cannot isolate the law from every other change.
  • Sodium and salt use different numbers: 2,000 mg sodium equals about 5 g salt, including salt already present in food.
  • Bread, processed meats, sauces and stock products can contribute salt even when a meal does not taste especially salty.
  • WHO's potassium-containing salt-substitute advice excludes people with impaired potassium excretion and does not apply to pregnant women or children.
  • For an older parent with kidney disease or potassium-raising medicines, check with the clinician before replacing table salt with a potassium product.
  • Read evidence quality and funding disclosures together; a commercial tie deserves scrutiny but does not substitute for assessing the study's methods and results.

❓ Questions to ask

Screenshot these or read them out.

  1. Mishka's prenatal has only 23 mg DHA, 10 mg choline and 6.5 µg vitamin D. Should she add a DHA supplement, and can we test vitamin D and iron (ferritin)?
  2. Is 15 mg of iron enough for her, based on her bloods? If we switched to Thorne Basic Prenatal (45 mg iron), would that be too much?
  3. Which strength of steroid cream should I use, on which body parts, and for how many days?
  4. Is my eczema bad enough for a dermatologist, or for biologic or JAK treatment, and does my medical aid cover it?
  5. What is my mom's target blood pressure, and how should she time her pills?
  6. Could any of her other medicines (like anti-inflammatory painkillers) be pushing her blood pressure up?
  7. Is this acne bad enough for isotretinoin, and what monitoring would that need?
  8. Which of my blood results are out of range, by how much, and do they need retesting?

⚠️ Watch out for

Myths, traps and red flags.

  • Myth: 'natural' prenatals are free of synthetic vitamins. Nearly all of them use lab-made forms, and some add herbs with no pregnancy safety data.
  • Megadoses are where the real risks are: vitamin C plus E, vitamin A (retinol) and possibly excess iron. One standard prenatal at the normal dose has no credible link to miscarriage or preterm labour.
  • Myth: steroid creams always thin your skin. Short courses at the right strength are safe; under-treating eczema causes more harm.
  • Red flag: eczema that suddenly weeps, crusts or forms painful clustered blisters, with fever. That can be infection or eczema herpeticum and needs a doctor the same day.
  • Myth: 'I feel fine, so I can stop my blood pressure pills.' High pressure has no feeling. Never stop without the doctor.
  • Red flag: severe headache, chest pain, sudden vision change, face drooping or slurred speech. Call 112 or 10177 now.
  • Myth: acne comes from dirty skin, and toothpaste or lemon juice fixes spots. Both irritate and can darken marks.
  • Hype: 'detox' teas, eczema 'cures' and supplements sold online. If it promises to cure eczema or blood pressure, be sceptical.
  • Wrist and finger BP gadgets and smartwatches are often inaccurate. Use a validated upper-arm cuff.
